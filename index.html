Every training vendor hands the hard half back to you

The NDIS Practice Standards expect relevant workers to be trained and, in key areas, assessed as competent. Read the fine print on any online course and you will find the same sentence: completion does not equal competency, and the provider remains responsible for supervision and competency assessment.

That is correct, and it is also the whole problem. A completion certificate proves someone clicked through fourteen modules. It is not evidence that the person can reason through a safeguarding decision at 9pm on a Sunday — and when a quality auditor asks how you know your workers can, a spreadsheet of tick-boxes is a thin answer.

We don't assess competence either — that decision is yours to make, and it should be. What we do is give you something to make it with: what each worker actually wrote, when they wrote it, and what the scenario was testing.

What a worker actually does

1. Read the situation

A short study guide, then a real support scenario — a participant wants to spend their money on something risky; a colleague's notes don't match what you saw; someone discloses something on shift.

2. Write the game plan

Not multiple choice. Not a memory test. In their own words: what would you do, in what order, and who would you tell?

3. The coach responds

What the plan covers gets a tick; what it misses gets coached — plus a supportive safety note if the plan drifts somewhere risky. A case is complete once the plan covers enough of what it calls for; until then the worker is shown exactly what to add and writes a fuller plan. No attempt limits, no score, and never marked wrong. People who are being graded write for the grader; people who are being coached think about the participant.

4. It's recorded

The submission, its date, and how much of what the scenario was testing they covered. Finish all six scenarios in a module and a Certificate of Completion is issued — a factual record of what they did, not a verdict on who they are. That is the artefact, and it's yours.

The document you hand the auditor

Admin → Evidence produces a printable pack for one worker or your whole organisation, with your name and logo on the cover:

  • Who completed what, and when — module by module, scenario by scenario
  • What each scenario was assessing, and how much of it they demonstrated
  • Certificate of Completion titles, serial numbers and award dates
  • Workers with no activity listed as such, rather than quietly left out
  • A CSV alongside it, for the people who live in spreadsheets

And not one word a worker wrote about a real person. The pack structurally cannot contain answer text — not by a rendering convention we promise to keep, but because the data never reaches the page that builds it, whoever generates it. Answers may contain personal reflections about real participants; they are treated as sensitive, and reading a single submission is a separate, deliberate action that is always recorded in the audit log.

What this is, plainly

This is non-accredited professional development. It is not nationally recognised training, it is not a qualification, and it does not make anyone a clinician. It is designed to complement the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission's free mandatory Worker Orientation Module — not to replace it. Every certificate says so on its face.

Workers are never scored, ranked, or marked wrong, and there are no attempt limits — a case completes when the plan covers enough of its practice points, and until then the coach shows what to add. Coverage statistics exist, but they are yours as a manager — a worker never sees a percentage.

Competence is your call. We give you dated written evidence and the audit trail around it. Deciding whether a worker is competent to work a shift is a supervision decision, and it stays with you.
